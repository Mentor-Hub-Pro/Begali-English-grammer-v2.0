1. Full Detailed Theory

Learning goal: Identify the job of every word in a sentence.

  1. Noun names; pronoun replaces; adjective describes; verb shows action/state.
  2. Adverb modifies a verb/adjective/adverb; preposition shows relationship.
  3. Conjunction joins ideas; interjection expresses a sudden feeling.
  4. Word class can change by context: “fast train” uses an adjective, “run fast” uses an adverb.
  5. A pronoun’s case depends on its job: subject before a verb, object after a verb/preposition.
  6. Preposition and phrasal-verb combinations are best learned as chunks, not isolated words.

Noun: the naming word

A noun is the name of a person, place, animal, thing, quality or idea. যার নাম আছে সেটাই Noun!

Pronoun: a noun’s helpful substitute

A pronoun replaces a noun, so we do not repeat the same name again and again.

Personal pronouns

Use subject forms before a verb and object forms after a verb or preposition.

  1. I read every day.
  2. She sings well.
  3. They play football.
  4. The teacher called me.
  5. Please help him.
  6. Rina saw us.
  7. This is my pen.
  8. That bag is hers.
  9. Our village is clean.
  10. The victory is theirs.

Reflexive and emphatic pronouns

Reflexive pronouns refer back to the subject; emphatic pronouns add stress.

  1. I hurt myself.
  2. She taught herself.
  3. They enjoyed themselves.
  4. We prepared ourselves.
  5. He blamed himself.
  6. I myself checked the answer.
  7. The principal himself spoke.
  8. She herself wrote the letter.
  9. We ourselves cleaned the room.
  10. They themselves solved it.

Demonstrative pronouns

This/these point to what is near; that/those point to what is farther away.

  1. This is my book.
  2. That is your chair.
  3. These are fresh flowers.
  4. Those are old trees.
  5. This is easy.
  6. That was a good idea.
  7. These belong to me.
  8. Those look expensive.
  9. This pen writes well.
  10. Those students are ready.

Indefinite pronouns

Indefinite pronouns refer to people or things without naming them exactly.

  1. Someone is at the door.
  2. Everyone is ready.
  3. Nobody knows the answer.

  6. Each has a pen.
  7. Many were absent.

Relative pronouns

Who is for people, which for things, that for people/things; whose shows possession.

  1. The boy who won is my friend.
  2. The book which I bought is useful.
  3. The girl whose pen is lost is crying.
  4. This is the house that I like.

  8. The child that smiled is happy.
  9. The student whose answer is correct won.

Who, whom, whose, which and what ask questions.

Adjective: the describing word

An adjective describes a noun or pronoun: its quality, number, quantity, position or ownership.

Types of adjective

Descriptive tells quality; numeral tells number/order; quantitative tells amount; demonstrative points out; interrogative asks; possessive shows ownership; proper comes from a proper noun.

  1. a beautiful flower (descriptive)
  2. three pens (numeral)
  3. little water (quantitative)
  4. this book (demonstrative)
  5. which road? (interrogative)
  6. my school (possessive)
  7. Indian food (proper)
  8. a brave soldier (descriptive)
  9. the first prize (numeral)
  10. much milk (quantitative)
  11. those houses (demonstrative)
  12. what colour? (interrogative)
  13. her dress (possessive)
  14. Bengali literature (proper)

Degrees: regular and irregular forms

Positive compares no degree, comparative compares two, superlative compares more than two. Short adjectives often take -er/-est; longer ones take more/most.

  1. tall → taller → tallest
  2. small → smaller → smallest
  3. happy → happier → happiest
  4. easy → easier → easiest
  5. big → bigger → biggest
  6. hot → hotter → hottest
  7. beautiful → more beautiful → most beautiful
  8. careful → more careful → most careful
  9. useful → more useful → most useful
  10. good → better → best
  11. bad → worse → worst
  12. little → less → least
  13. much/many → more → most
  14. far → farther/further → farthest/furthest
  15. old → older/elder → oldest/eldest
  16. late → later → latest
  17. wise → wiser → wisest
  18. brave → braver → bravest
  19. busy → busier → busiest
  20. important → more important → most important

Opinion, Size, Age, Shape, Colour, Origin, Material, Purpose, Noun. Do not pile adjectives randomly.

Verb: the action or state word

A verb tells what someone does, is, has or feels. Without a finite verb, a full English clause cannot stand.

Types of verb

Main verbs carry meaning; auxiliaries help them. Transitive verbs take objects; intransitive do not; linking verbs connect the subject with a description.

  1. She writes a letter. (main)
  2. He is reading. (auxiliary + main)
  3. They have finished. (auxiliary + main)
  4. Rina reads a book. (transitive)
  5. The baby sleeps. (intransitive)
  6. The soup tastes good. (linking)
  7. She became happy. (linking)
  8. We made a plan. (transitive)
  9. Birds fly. (intransitive)
  10. I do my work. (main)
  11. They are ready. (linking)
  12. He has a bicycle. (main)

Finite and non-finite verbs

A finite verb changes with tense/subject. Non-finite forms do not: infinitive (to + V1), gerund (V-ing as noun), participle (V-ing/V3 as adjective).

  1. She writes daily. (finite)
  2. They wrote yesterday. (finite)
  3. He goes to school. (finite)
  4. I want to learn. (infinitive)
  5. To err is human. (infinitive)
  6. She likes to sing. (infinitive)
  7. Swimming is good exercise. (gerund)
  8. Reading improves knowledge. (gerund)
  9. He enjoys playing. (gerund)
  10. The crying baby slept. (present participle)
  11. A broken glass lay there. (past participle)
  12. The smiling girl waved. (present participle)

Adverb: how, when, where and how much

An adverb modifies a verb, adjective or another adverb. It tells manner, time, place, frequency, degree or affirmation.

It confirms or denies an idea.

A conjunction joins words, phrases or clauses. An interjection suddenly expresses a feeling.

For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So join equal ideas.
