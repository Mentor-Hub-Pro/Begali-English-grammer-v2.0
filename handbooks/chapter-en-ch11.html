1. Full Detailed Theory

Learning goal: Write clear English with correct punctuation, spelling and word order.

  1. Punctuation guides meaning; capitals mark proper names and sentence starts.
  2. English normal order is Subject + Verb + Object + Adverbial.
  3. Negative adverbs at the start require inversion.
  4. Punctuation can change meaning: commas separate units, apostrophes show possession/contraction, and quotation marks show exact words.
  5. Spelling memory improves through sound, syllable, word family and repeated written recall.
  6. Inversion is exceptional; use normal SVOA order unless a negative/limiting expression requires an auxiliary before the subject.

Punctuation and Capital Letters

Punctuation guides a reader through your meaning. A correct comma, apostrophe or question mark can change a sentence from confusing to clear.

Full stop (.)

Use a full stop after a complete statement or polite imperative.

  1. Rina reads every day.
  2. Please close the door.
  3. The train arrived late.
  4. We live in Kolkata.
  5. The lesson is over.

Comma (,): 10 essential rules

A comma separates items, introductory words, clauses, direct address, and non-essential information. Do not put a comma between a subject and its verb.

  1. I bought pens, pencils, books and paper.
  2. After dinner, we went for a walk.
  3. Yes, I understand.
  4. Rina, please come here.
  5. My brother, who lives in Delhi, is a doctor.
  6. If it rains, we will stay home.
  7. However, the plan failed.
  8. On Monday, we will begin.
  9. The old man, tired and weak, sat down.
  10. No, thank you.

Semicolon (;)

Use a semicolon between closely related independent clauses or in a complex list.

  1. The rain stopped; we started walking.
  2. She studied hard; she passed.
  3. The road was long; the journey was pleasant.
  4. We need pens, paper and ink; books and files; and chairs.
  5. He was tired; however, he continued.

Colon (:)

Use a colon before a list, explanation or quotation after a complete clause.

  1. Bring these items: a pen, a ruler and a notebook.
  2. She had one aim: to succeed.
  3. The notice said: “Keep silence.”
  4. There are three colours: red, blue and green.
  5. Remember this rule: practise daily.

Apostrophe (’)

Use for possession and contraction. Do not use it for ordinary plurals.

  1. Rina’s book is new.
  2. The boys’ bags are outside.
  3. The children’s toys are here.
  4. It’s raining.
  5. The dog wagged its tail.

Inverted commas (“ ”)

Use to show direct speech or exact quoted words.

  1. He said, “I am ready.”
  2. “Stop!” shouted the guard.
  3. The word “honesty” is an abstract noun.
  4. Mother asked, “Where are you going?”
  5. She said, “Please help me.”

Question mark (?)

Use after a direct question, not after an indirect question.

  1. Where do you live?
  2. Did you finish the work?
  3. Can I help you?
  4. He asked where I lived.
  5. Why are you late?

Exclamation mark (!)

Use for strong feeling, command or interjection; use it sparingly.

  1. Hurrah! We won.
  2. Alas! He is dead.
  3. What a beautiful view!
  4. Stop!
  5. How clever she is!

Hyphen (-)

Use in compound modifiers and some compound words.

  1. a well-known writer
  2. a five-year-old child
  3. a twenty-page report
  4. mother-in-law
  5. a part-time job

Dash (—)

Use a dash for an abrupt break or an added emphatic idea.

  1. One thing is certain—hard work matters.
  2. She opened the box—and gasped.
  3. The result was clear—everyone passed.
  4. He had one goal—success.
  5. The road was empty—completely empty.

Capital Letters: 10 rules

Capitalise the first word of a sentence, proper nouns, days/months, languages, nationalities, titles before names and the pronoun I.

  1. Rina reads daily. (start of sentence)
  2. Kolkata is in India. (proper places)
  3. Monday comes after Sunday. (days)
  4. We visit in December. (months)
  5. She speaks Bengali and English. (languages)
  6. He is an Indian student. (nationality)
  7. Dr Sen is a doctor. (title before name)
  8. I am ready. (pronoun I)
  9. The Ganga is a river. (named river)
  10. Rabindranath wrote Gitanjali. (person and book title)

Word Order and Syntax

English depends strongly on word order. Put the subject before the verb in normal statements; use inversion only in special patterns.

SVOA word order: 10 examples

Basic English order is Subject + Verb + Object + Adverbial. Keep the main verb close to its subject.

  1. Rina reads books every day.
  2. The farmer grows rice in the field.
  3. We play football after school.
  4. My father drives the car carefully.
  5. The teacher explains the lesson clearly.
  6. They visited Kolkata last year.
  7. The child drank milk quickly.
  8. I will meet you tomorrow.
  9. Birds build nests in spring.
  10. The doctor examined the patient carefully.

Inversion after negative adverbs: 15 examples

After never, seldom, rarely, hardly, scarcely, no sooner, not only, little—put auxiliary before subject.

  1. Never have I seen such a view.
  2. Seldom does she complain.
  3. Rarely do we get such rain.
  4. Hardly had he arrived when it began to rain.
  5. Scarcely had I sat down when the bell rang.
  6. No sooner had he come than we started.
  7. Not only did she sing, but she also danced.
  8. Little did I know the truth.
  9. Nowhere can you find such peace.
  10. Under no circumstances should you lie.
  11. Only then did I understand.
  12. At no time did he complain.
  13. Barely had we left when it rained.
  14. Never will I forget you.
  15. Rarely has the team played so well.

OSASCOMP: adjective order

Opinion + Size + Age + Shape + Colour + Origin + Material + Purpose + Noun.

  1. a beautiful small old red Italian wooden table
  2. a lovely large new round blue plastic box
  3. an expensive little black leather bag
  4. a nice big old Bengali house
  5. a beautiful long white cotton dress
  6. a small square metal tin
  7. an ugly old brown wooden chair
  8. a smart young Indian student
  9. a large modern glass building
  10. a lovely red silk scarf
Shortcut: Long sentence লিখলে আগে ছোট SVO বাক্য বানাও, তারপর time/place phrase যোগ করো। এতে word order নিজে থেকেই ঠিক থাকে।
Memory trick: Subject first, verb next, object then; adverbial comes at the end.
Common mistakes:
  • ✗ Every day reads Rina books. → ✓ Rina reads books every day.
  • ✗ Never I have seen this. → ✓ Never have I seen this.
  • ✗ No sooner he had come than… → ✓ No sooner had he come than…
  • ✗ a red beautiful dress → ✓ a beautiful red dress
  • ✗ She quickly the work finished. → ✓ She finished the work quickly.
Exam focus:
  • Arrange: “every day / reads / Rina / books” → Rina reads books every day.
  • Inversion: “Never ___ I seen it.” → have.
  • Correct order → a beautiful red silk sari.
