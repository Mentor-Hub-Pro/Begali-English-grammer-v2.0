1. Full Detailed Theory

Learning goal: Read a passage for evidence, not for every single word.

  1. Main idea combines opening and conclusion.
  2. Detail answers are stated; inference answers logically follow.
  3. Context gives the best clue for vocabulary meaning.
  4. Skimming finds topic and structure; scanning finds a named fact, number or keyword.
  5. Inference means the safest logical conclusion, not a creative guess beyond evidence.
  6. Distractor options may copy a passage word but change the relationship, quantity or reason.

Reading Comprehension: 5-step smart technique

A comprehension passage tests reading, not memorised facts. Read with a purpose: find topic, structure, evidence, word meaning and conclusion.

Five steps

Use a simple time formula: 2 minutes overview + 1 minute questions + 5 minutes evidence + 2 minutes review.

  1. Step 1: Read the title and first sentence to identify the topic.
  2. Step 2: Notice connectors—because, however, therefore, although.
  3. Step 3: Read each question and underline its keyword.
  4. Step 4: Return to the exact paragraph for evidence.
  5. Step 5: Eliminate options that are too broad, too narrow or opposite.

Five question-type strategies

Main idea, detail, vocabulary, inference and tone each need a slightly different reading move.

  1. Main idea: combine opening and conclusion.
  2. Detail: locate the exact fact, number or name.
  3. Vocabulary: read one sentence before and after.
  4. Inference: choose only what logically follows.
  5. Tone/purpose: notice whether the writer explains, warns, praises or compares.
Shortcut: If two options look close, choose the one that matches the passage most completely—not the one that merely sounds sensible.
Memory trick: Topic, clue, evidence, choice; read with purpose, use your voice.
Common mistakes:
  • ✗ Answering from general knowledge → ✓ Answering from the written passage
  • ✗ Ignoring however/but → ✓ Treating contrast words as important turns
  • ✗ Spending too long on one question → ✓ Marking and returning after other answers
Exam focus:
  • A detail question asks “what does the passage say?”; an inference asks “what follows from it?”
  • Time management formula: do not sacrifice four easy questions for one difficult word.

Passage 1: Panchayati Raj

Read the passage: Panchayati Raj brings government closer to rural people. Instead of waiting for decisions from distant offices, villagers can discuss local needs through elected representatives and Gram Sabha meetings. Clean drinking water, village roads, drainage, primary schools, health centres and work opportunities are common concerns. A good Panchayat does not merely spend money; it listens to people, prepares a clear plan, keeps records open and checks the quality of completed work. Participation matters greatly. Women, elderly people, farmers, workers and persons with disabilities may experience a village differently, so their voices improve a plan. Citizens also have duties: they should attend meetings, protect public property, avoid spreading false information and ask respectful questions. When people and representatives work together honestly, small improvements become lasting development. Panchayati Raj therefore teaches an important democratic lesson: development is strongest when it is planned with people, not only for people.

Passage 2: Environment

Read the passage: The environment is not an extra part of life; it is the system that supports life itself. Air, water, soil, plants and animals are connected in ways that people do not always notice. When a pond is polluted, fish suffer first, but people who depend on that water may also suffer later. When trees are cut without planning, the soil can lose moisture and birds can lose shelter. Climate change makes this connection even clearer. Unusual heat, irregular rainfall and stronger storms affect crops, health and daily work. The solution is not only a large government project. Small habits matter too: saving water, carrying a reusable bag, separating waste, planting local trees and switching off unused lights. Schools can encourage students to observe their surroundings and take part in cleaning drives. Real environmental care does not mean refusing all development. It means choosing development that uses resources wisely and leaves a safe, healthy world for the next generation.

Passage 4: Rural Technology

Read the passage: Technology can improve rural life when it is simple, affordable and suited to local needs. A solar lamp, for example, can help a student study after sunset in a village with an uncertain electricity supply. A mobile phone can give farmers weather information and market prices. Small machines can reduce hard physical work, while digital records can make public services more transparent. However, technology alone cannot solve every problem. People need training to use a device safely and confidently. Repair facilities, local language instructions and reliable networks are also necessary. If a machine is too costly or cannot be repaired nearby, it may remain unused. The best rural technology is therefore not always the newest technology. It is the technology that local people can understand, maintain and adapt. When communities take part in choosing tools, technology becomes a partner in development rather than an object imposed from outside.

Passage 5: Women Empowerment

Read the passage: Women’s empowerment means that women have the knowledge, confidence and opportunity to make decisions about their own lives. Education is central to this process because it opens doors to work, health awareness and participation in public life. When a girl stays in school, she is more likely to understand her rights and contribute to her family’s income in the future. Yet many barriers remain. Poverty, early marriage, unsafe travel, household duties and old ideas about gender can interrupt education. Communities can reduce these barriers by ensuring safe transport, clean school facilities, scholarships and supportive families. Role models are also powerful. Seeing women succeed as teachers, engineers, farmers, athletes, entrepreneurs and public representatives changes what young people believe is possible. Empowerment does not mean replacing one group’s voice with another’s. It means ensuring equal respect and equal opportunity. A society that supports women’s education and leadership uses the full strength of its people.
